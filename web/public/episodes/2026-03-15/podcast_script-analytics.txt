For those unfamiliar, prompt injection is a serious security vulnerability where a user can manipulate an LLM into ignoring its original instructions or performing unintended actions by crafting a clever input prompt.

Dana: So, like, if I've built a customer service bot designed to only answer questions about product features, a malicious user could potentially "inject" a prompt that makes it reveal sensitive internal information or even generate harmful content?

Amir: Precisely. It's like giving an instruction to a sophisticated tool, but then someone whispering a contradictory, higher-priority instruction right after, causing the tool to deviate from its intended purpose. The Databricks blog post details various strategies to combat this, which is crucial because, unlike traditional software vulnerabilities, prompt injection exploits the inherent flexibility and generative nature of LLMs themselves.

Dana: So, what kind of mitigations are we talking about? Is this something Databricks is building in at a platform level, or are they giving us best practices to implement ourselves?

Amir: It’s a bit of both, but primarily it’s about equipping developers with best practices and tools. The guidance often revolves around several layers of defense. This includes robust input validation to filter out suspicious patterns before they even reach the LLM, implementing guardrails and "safety layers" post-LLM output, and even using techniques like "system prompts" and "few-shot examples" to strongly condition the LLM's behavior. They also touch on the importance of fine-tuning and using smaller, more controlled models for specific tasks where possible, to reduce the surface area for injection.
